A Map of Nowhere

My grandfather kept a map of a place he never went. It hung in the hall, framed,
a coastline of some northern country with the towns marked in a language he could
not read.

I asked him about it once. He said he had bought it at a stall for the price of a
coffee, forty years back, because the shape of the coast pleased him. That was the
whole reason. No plan to travel there, no ancestor from those hills, nothing but a
line on paper that his eye liked to follow.

I understood him better later than I did then. We are told to want things for
reasons, to justify our small attachments with a story. But the map needed no
story. It was a good line, and he looked at it every day for forty years, and when
he died I took it down and found the wall behind it a paler square than the rest,
the paint saved from the sun by four decades of a coast he never walked.

The map is mine now. It hangs in my hall. I still cannot read the town names, and
I have stopped trying, and I follow the coast with my eye the way he must have,
going nowhere, arriving anyway.
